Christopher M. Sullivan

Associate Professor of Political Science · Louisiana State University

How is political order produced, contested, and enforced? I answer with the records that coercive institutions keep about themselves.

The aim is practical: to understand when polarized societies tip into violence, how they pull back, and what repression leaves behind.

Email CV Google Scholar Data LSU

I study the micro-foundations of political conflict: how states repress, how movements respond, and how political order emerges from the contest between them. My evidence comes from police, army, party, and intelligence files from Guatemala, Northern Ireland, the United States, and Iraq, paired with research designs built for causal inference and aimed at questions of public interest.

I am a tenured Associate Professor in the Department of Political Science at Louisiana State University, where I have taught since 2015. My work sits at the intersection of comparative politics, international relations, and political sociology and has appeared in World Politics, the Journal of Politics, Nature Human Behaviour, the Journal of Conflict Resolution, and the Journal of Peace Research, among others. It is currently supported by a Smith Richardson Foundation Strategy and Policy Fellowship, for my book on the origins of political order in Guatemala, and by the Hoover Institution's Silas Palmer Fellowship, for new research on intelligence and mass violence in Ba'athist Iraq. I began working in Guatemala's National Police Historical Archive in 2009, a few years after its discovery, and I have since conducted archival field research in Latin America, Europe, and the United States.

Research

One question, three themes

A single question runs through my research: how is political order produced, contested, and enforced? I pursue it through three connected themes. The first looks at the state and how repression works; the second at challengers and how they adapt; the third at outcomes and whether coercion delivers what it promises. All three rest on the same foundation: the records that coercive institutions keep about themselves, turned into event data and analyzed with designs that can tell cause from coincidence. Three new directions now grow out of them: a study of intelligence and massacres in Ba'athist Iraq, a new project on backlash with Emily Hencken Ritter, and an approach to public opinion that treats the ideals people hold as products of contentious politics.

Theme 1 · The state

Enforcing order: how repression works

How does repression work, and why don't people rebel?

Most people, most of the time, comply with regimes that have not earned their consent. Order under repressive rule is the puzzle; rebellion is the exception. Decades of research found that repression sometimes suppresses dissent and sometimes inflames it, without settling why.

The Guatemalan National Police kept files on the people they watched, detained, and killed. Reading those files as data—event by event, organization by organization, from 1975 to 1985—shows a state that repressed selectively and pre-emptively, and reveals which kinds of coercion dismantled opposition and which fed it. Coercion aimed at an organization's clandestine work dismantles dissent; coercion aimed at overt protest tends to provoke it. The same records anchor my book, Why Not Rebel?, which traces how a political order was rebuilt after the worst of the state's violence and follows its legacies into the civil society, protests, and elections of the decades that followed.

What I've found

  • Repressing clandestine organizational work—meetings, recruitment, training, fundraising—sharply reduces later dissent; repressing overt collective action provokes more of it.
  • Police concentrate coercion on the mobilization that precedes open challenge, and on groups making radical demands. Enforcement begins before anyone takes to the street.
  • When the 1976 earthquake degraded the state's capacity to watch, police lost the ability to pre-empt and fell back on reactive violence.
  • State massacres follow a logic of removing threats and asserting territorial control.

What it changed

  • Moved the unit of analysis from the protest to the organization: the organizational labor behind protest is where repression bites, and where it should be measured.
  • Resolved part of the repression–dissent puzzle by distinguishing what is being repressed.
  • Brought surveillance into the study of repression: the capacity to watch shapes when the state resorts to violence.

Theme 2 · Challengers

Resisting order: how movements adapt

How do movements adapt to coercion and surveillance, and when does repression backfire?

Challengers are not passive targets. Movements facing coercion change shape, and the backlash repression provokes is carried by particular people and particular organizations rather than by a movement as a whole.

Using records produced by both sides of a conflict—police intelligence units and the organizations they watched—this work follows, week by week, how challengers reorganize under pressure. The same approach, applied to the Northern Ireland Research Initiative's thirty years of event data, is now turning to a question with direct relevance for the United States: how divided places lose trust in the state, when that distrust turns violent, and how trust is won back.

What I've found

  • Overt and covert repression work at cross purposes: raids and arrests reveal what the state knows, and challengers adapt in ways that blind future surveillance.
  • Backlash runs at two levels at once. Organizations grow more active after repression; individually, only those personally repressed step up, while bystanders withdraw.
  • In Northern Ireland, repression raised dissent when dissent was already falling and lowered it when dissent was rising.

What it changed

  • Treated challengers as strategic adapters rather than targets, and showed that surveillance and overt coercion can undercut each other.
  • Disaggregated backlash to the individual, reconciling deterrence and backlash as simultaneous effects on different people.
  • Established the organizational approach to state repression that anchored the NSF-funded Northern Ireland project.

Theme 3 · Outcomes

Evaluating coercion: does it work?

Do coercive policies deliver what they promise?

Torture, aggressive policing, and even humanitarian aid are defended on the grounds that they reduce violence. Those claims can be tested. This theme brings design-based inference—natural experiments, matching, difference-in-differences—to the question of whether coercion achieves what its advocates say.

The answers have reached well beyond the discipline, through Nature Human Behaviour and coverage in outlets from the Los Angeles Times to Ars Technica, putting evidence into public arguments about torture and policing.

What I've found

  • In Guatemala, torture was followed by more counterinsurgent killing and no robust reduction in insurgent violence.
  • When the NYPD sharply curtailed proactive enforcement in 2014–15, complaints of major crime fell during and after the slowdown.
  • Humanitarian aid in African civil wars is associated with more rebel violence against civilians.

What it changed

  • Replaced assertion with identification: each claim is tested with a design that separates cause from coincidence.
  • Showed that the questions of conflict research—does coercion reduce violence?—apply as directly to policing in New York as to counterinsurgency in Guatemala.

Foundation · Method

Conflict archives as data

What can the records of coercive institutions tell us that surveys and newspapers cannot?

Combatants write things down. Police, armies, ministries, and rebel groups generate memos, reports, and rosters, and more of these records are opened every year. Turning them into systematic data, and understanding their biases, has been central to my work since my first months in the Guatemalan police archive in 2009.

What it changed

  • Among the first scholars to work in Guatemala's National Police Historical Archive, some 80 million pages discovered in 2005.
  • Co-edited the Journal of Peace Research special issue that set out a research agenda and methods for archival conflict data.
  • Principal investigator, with Christian Davenport and Cyanne Loyle, of the Northern Ireland Research Initiative, a georeferenced, multi-source events database on the Troubles.
  • Documented how source selection shapes what police data can and cannot show.

The Even Hand

How divided places lose and regain trust in the state, from Belfast to America

After every wave of protest, Americans on the left and right make the same complaint: the police arrest our protesters and let the other side's go free. A divided country does not turn violent because its people disagree. It turns violent when one side concludes that the state has taken the other's part and stops trusting it.

That distrust shows first on particular streets. Residents of one neighborhood stop obeying the government and govern themselves, with patrols and barricades, while people of the same community a few streets away do not. I call this contested authority: distrust turned into rival rule, and the link between what the state does and the violence that follows. My theory contends that a divided society heals when the state shows an even hand, treating like conduct alike on both sides, and opens a door, offering concessions to those shut out.

Northern Ireland lived the full arc from lost trust to recovery, and the Northern Ireland Research Initiative, which I built with Christian Davenport and Cyanne Loyle under a National Science Foundation grant, records it street by street. This project adds the police and army records that show whom the state arrested, square kilometer by square kilometer, and uses the British Army's battalion rotations, set on a roster months ahead, to compare the same streets under units that policed evenly and units that did not.

What it will tell us

Four tests ask how trust breaks, how concessions buy it back, how it returns after violence, and whether the pattern holds in U.S. counties since 2020. The findings become a book for general readers, The Even Hand; an online atlas of trust and policing in Northern Ireland; a U.S. county map of policing by conduct; and three one-page decision cards, for police chiefs, for mayors and governors, and for mediators, each stating one practice and the evidence behind it.

Why Not Rebel?

The Micro-Foundations of Political Order

Guatemala's civil war produced one of the most extreme campaigns of state violence in the Western Hemisphere. Then the mass killing ended, years before the war formally did, and a political order took hold that most Guatemalans had not chosen and few openly resisted. Why Not Rebel? asks how.

Drawing on more than 100,000 records of the National Police that I hand-coded over two years in an archive of eighty million pages, and using the 1976 earthquake as a shock to the state's capacity to watch, the book shows that repression produces order not by containing violence but by dismantling the clandestine organizational work on which open challenge depends, and by reshaping the ideological composition of local civil society. Its later chapters follow the legacies of that repression into municipal and national elections since 1996 and into the demands of protests decades later, measured against the demands for redistribution communities made before the conflict. Rather than asking why people rebel, it asks why, so often, they do not, and finds the answer in the organizational life that repression targets rather than in the attitudes of individuals or the strength of the state alone. What we call peace, it argues, can be the successful narrowing of what communities are able to demand.

Bureaucracy and Brutality

Intelligence and state-sponsored massacres in Iraq, 1987–1989

Why do states commit massacres where and when they do? Cross-national research has treated mass killing as a single calculus made by national agencies responding to pre-existing threats. This project argues instead that states first construct threats and then respond to them: surveillance reaches further into some regions than others, information gathered locally filters upward unevenly, and as intelligence agencies sift their data, entire communities can be labeled threats and selected as targets.

The evidence comes from the Ba'ath Party records in the Hoover Institution's Northern Iraq Database, seized by Kurdish forces in 1991 and covering the regime's most violent years. Produced for bureaucratic performance rather than public consumption, they are among the most unfiltered records of a state security apparatus available anywhere. From them I am building two linked datasets, on where and when massacres took place and on the transfer of intelligence about potential threats, with qualitative evidence of the causal process extracted from the Arabic originals. The project extends the organizational approach from the policing of dissent to the gravest form of state violence, and it ties my early work on massacres in Guatemala to the state's informational capacity.

And the Heat Goes On: Police Repression and the Modalities of Power

Howard Liu and Christopher M. Sullivan

Journal of Conflict Resolution 65(10): 1657–1679, 2021

Enforcing order
JournalPDF

Police do two things: they watch, and they strike. Drawing on Michael Mann's distinction between infrastructural and despotic power, the article treats Guatemala's 1976 earthquake as a shock to the state's capacity to surveil. Where damage was greatest, police lost track of emerging movements, dissidents found room to mobilize, and the police response turned harsher and more reactive. Violence receded as the state regained control.

New Findings from Conflict Archives: An Introduction and Methodological Framework

Laia Balcells and Christopher M. Sullivan

Journal of Peace Research 55(2): 137–146, 2018. Introduction to the special issue New Findings from Conflict Archives, co-edited with Laia Balcells

Archives as data
Special issue

Combatants produce and keep records, and more of them are released after conflicts every year. The introduction builds a research agenda around their systematic analysis: new methods for sampling, cataloging, and analyzing documents, and a reckoning with the biases these sources carry. The contributions show that violence interacts with factors conventional conflict data rarely capture, among them clandestine mobilization, bureaucratic accountability, and political identity.

Resistance Is Mobile: Dynamics of Repression, Challenger Adaptation, and Surveillance in US "Red Squad" and Black Nationalist Archives

Christopher M. Sullivan and Christian Davenport

Journal of Peace Research 55(2): 175–189, 2018

Resisting order
Journal

A common view holds that defeating a movement requires combining overt repression (raids, arrests) with covert repression (monitoring, informants, wiretaps). Weekly panel data on the Republic of New Africa and the police units that watched it, 1968–1971, show the two can work at cross purposes. Overt repression reveals what the state knows, and challengers respond by shifting to less observable tactics and forms, making the next round of intelligence harder to gather.

Evidence that Curtailing Proactive Policing Can Reduce Major Crime

Christopher M. Sullivan and Zachary P. O'Keeffe

Nature Human Behaviour 1(10): 730–737, 2017

JournalPDFCoverage

Does aggressive enforcement of minor offenses reduce serious crime? Police deployment and citizen behavior shape each other, which makes the question hard to answer. In late 2014 and early 2015 the New York Police Department effectively suspended proactive policing. Using several years of NYPD data, the article finds that complaints of major crimes such as burglary, felony assault, and grand larceny fell during and shortly after the slowdown, contrary to the assumptions behind broken-windows policing.

Political Repression and the Destruction of Dissident Organizations: Evidence from the Archives of the Guatemalan National Police

Christopher M. Sullivan

World Politics 68(4): 645–676, 2016

Enforcing order
Journal

Why does repression sometimes crush dissent and sometimes inflame it? Police records from Guatemala, 1975–1985, separate two targets. Repression aimed at clandestine organizational work (meetings, training, recruitment, fundraising) sharply reduces subsequent dissent. Repression aimed at overt collective action provokes more. The inconsistency in the literature reflects the failure to distinguish what is being repressed.
